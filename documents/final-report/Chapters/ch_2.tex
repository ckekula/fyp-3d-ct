\subsection{BioMedParse}

BiomedParse is a foundation model for segmentation, detection, and recognition that extends prompt driven parsing to medical imaging tasks \cite{zhao2025foundation}.
Rather than relying on a fixed label set during inference, the model accepts a natural language prompt describing the target anatomy or pathology and produces a pixel level mask for the queried concept.
This makes BiomedParse especially useful in settings where the structures of interest are heterogeneous, difficult to enumerate in advance, or described differently across institutions and reports.

In the context of chest CT, BiomedParse is attractive because many clinically relevant findings such as lung nodules, lung opacity, consolidation, and atelectasis can be queried directly with disease specific text prompts.
The model combines image encoding with language understanding and uses a transformer based decoder to align the prompt with the visual features extracted from the scan \cite{zhao2025foundation}. 
The output is not only a segmentation map but also a form of interpretable localization, since the generated mask indicates where the model believes the prompted abnormality is present.
Therefore, BiomedParse serves both as a SotA reference model and as a practical contrast for discussing performance, localization quality, and interpretability in 3D chest CT analysis.


\subsection{TotalSegmentator}

Accurate anatomical segmentation is an important step in CT-based
computer-aided diagnosis (CAD). Earlier deep learning methods were mainly
developed to segment only a few organs. To solve this problem, Wasserthal
\textit{et al.} introduced \textbf{TotalSegmentator}, a deep learning framework
that can automatically segment 104 anatomical structures from a single CT scan
\cite{wasserthal2023totalsegmentator}. The framework is built on the
\textit{nnU-Net} architecture, which automatically adjusts the training process
to different datasets. This helps the model achieve reliable segmentation on
different clinical CT images.

The model was trained using more than 1,200 routine clinical CT scans collected
from different hospitals and patient groups. It achieved a mean Dice Similarity
Coefficient (DSC) of about 0.94, showing high segmentation accuracy. Because of
its good performance, TotalSegmentator is widely used as a preprocessing tool in
medical image analysis, including computer-aided diagnosis, surgical planning,
and radiotherapy planning \cite{wasserthal2023totalsegmentator}.

    \caption{Example of automatic multi-organ segmentation produced by TotalSegmentator on a CT scan (adapted from \cite{wasserthal2023totalsegmentator}).}
    \label{fig:totalsegmentator}

Although TotalSegmentator provides accurate anatomical segmentation, it is not
designed to classify pulmonary abnormalities such as lung nodules or
ground-glass opacities. It also does not explain how its predictions are made.
Therefore, additional methods are needed for disease classification and
interpretable clinical decision-making.

The proposed research aims to overcome these limitations by developing an
interpretable Label Consistent K-SVD (LC-KSVD) framework for pulmonary
abnormality classification and grounded localization. While TotalSegmentator
provides useful anatomical information, the proposed framework focuses on
improving both classification performance and the interpretability of the
results.

\subsection{CT-CLIP}

CT images contain valuable information for diagnosing pulmonary diseases, while
radiology reports provide detailed clinical descriptions. To learn information
from both sources, Hamamci \textit{et al.} proposed \textbf{CT-CLIP}, a
vision-language foundation model for chest CT images
\cite{hamamci2024ctclip}. Unlike traditional supervised models that require
large amounts of labeled data, CT-CLIP learns the relationship between CT scans
and their corresponding radiology reports using contrastive learning. This
allows the model to learn meaningful image and text representations without
task-specific annotations.

CT-CLIP consists of a 3D image encoder and a text encoder. The image encoder
extracts features from CT volumes, while the text encoder processes the
corresponding radiology reports. During training, the model learns to match
each CT scan with its correct report in a shared feature space. The model was
trained on thousands of chest CT scans paired with radiology reports, allowing
it to learn both anatomical and pathological information. It has shown strong
performance in pulmonary disease classification, image retrieval, and zero-shot
learning tasks \cite{hamamci2024ctclip}.

Although CT-CLIP achieves good performance, it mainly learns high-level feature
representations instead of directly localizing pulmonary abnormalities. It also
does not provide inherent explanations for its predictions, making the decision
process difficult to understand.

The proposed research aims to overcome these limitations by developing an
interpretable Label Consistent K-SVD (LC-KSVD) framework for pulmonary
abnormality classification and grounded localization. While CT-CLIP learns rich
image and text representations, the proposed framework focuses on providing
transparent and interpretable diagnostic results.





\section{Explainable AI (XAI) in Medical Imaging}

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become an important tool in medical imaging by
supporting tasks such as disease detection, classification, and image analysis.
Many deep learning models achieve high accuracy, but their decision-making
process is often difficult to understand. These models are commonly referred to
as "black-box" models because they do not clearly explain how a prediction is
made \cite{samek2017explainable,guidotti2018survey}. In healthcare, this lack of
transparency can reduce clinicians' confidence in AI-assisted diagnosis.

Interpretability is important because medical decisions directly affect patient
care. Clinicians need to understand why a model predicts a particular disease
before using its results in clinical practice. Explainable AI (XAI) provides
methods that help users understand how AI models make decisions. This improves
trust, supports clinical decision-making, and helps identify possible model
errors or biases \cite{tjoa2021survey}.

The importance of XAI has also increased due to ethical and regulatory
requirements. Healthcare AI systems are expected to be transparent, reliable,
and accountable before they are used in real clinical environments.
Explainable models help meet these requirements by providing evidence that
supports their predictions instead of producing results without explanation
\cite{guidotti2018survey,tjoa2021survey}.

Explainability is especially important for chest CT diagnosis because pulmonary
abnormalities such as lung nodules and ground-glass opacities can be difficult
to identify. Clinicians need to know which regions of the CT scan influenced the
model's prediction. This helps verify the diagnosis and increases confidence in
AI-assisted clinical decisions. Therefore, improving interpretability has become
an important research direction in computer-aided diagnosis systems
\cite{tjoa2021survey}.


\subsection{SHAP}

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a widely used explainable artificial
intelligence (XAI) method for interpreting machine learning models. It is based
on Shapley values from cooperative game theory, where each feature is assigned
an importance value according to its contribution to the final prediction. This
provides a consistent and theoretically sound approach for explaining both
traditional machine learning and deep learning models
\cite{lundberg2017unified}.

One of the main advantages of SHAP is that it provides both global and local
explanations. Global explanations identify the features that have the greatest
overall influence on model predictions across the entire dataset. Local
explanations show how each feature contributes to the prediction of an
individual sample. SHAP values can be positive or negative, indicating whether
a feature increases or decreases the predicted outcome. These explanations help
users understand why a model makes a particular decision and improve confidence
in AI-based systems \cite{lundberg2017unified,tjoa2021survey}.

In medical imaging, SHAP has been widely used to improve the transparency and
interpretability of artificial intelligence models. It helps researchers and
clinicians identify the imaging features and clinical variables that have the
greatest influence on model predictions. This makes it easier to determine
whether the model is learning meaningful medical information rather than
irrelevant patterns. As a result, SHAP supports the development of more
reliable and trustworthy computer-aided diagnosis systems
\cite{holzinger2019causability,tjoa2021survey}.

For pulmonary disease analysis using CT images, SHAP can explain the factors
that influence a model's predictions by measuring the contribution of each
input feature. These explanations help clinicians understand AI-assisted
decisions, verify whether the model focuses on clinically relevant information,
and identify possible prediction errors. Therefore, SHAP improves the
interpretability, reliability, and clinical acceptance of deep learning models
used for pulmonary disease diagnosis
\cite{lundberg2017unified,tjoa2021survey}.
