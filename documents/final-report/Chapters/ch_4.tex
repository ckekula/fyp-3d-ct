\section{Sparse-Coding and Dictionary Learning}

Our study included four dictionary-learning methods: K-SVD, LC-KSVD, FDDL, and Frozen Dictionary Learning.
Each method was evaluated on the same test split of patches, and their reconstruction quality was assessed.
Table~\ref{tab:dictionary_comparison} compares the reconstruction error and mutual incoherence of each method.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Comparison of dictionary learning methods for reconstruction quality.}
\label{tab:dictionary_comparison}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Reconstruction Error} & \textbf{Mutual Incoherence} \\
\hline
K-SVD                       & 0.1037     & 0.7414 \\
LC-KSVD                     & 3.1457     & 0.2318 \\
FDDL                        & 1.2958     & 0.0190 \\
Frozen Dictionary Learning  & 0.0880     & 0.6702 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Here, a lower Mutual incoherence means that the atoms are more distinct from each other and thus,
uniqeuly contribute to the reconstruction process.
LC-KSVD and FDDL's reconstruction error columns are their joint-training objective (reconstruction
combined with a label-consistency or Fisher-discrimination term) rather than a pure reconstruction
error, so those two values are not directly comparable to K-SVD and Frozen DL.
The Frozen DL error and mutual incoherence terms shown are the average values of its three per-stage errors.
The per-class values are shown in table~\ref{tab:frozen-reconstruction}.

Since separate KSVD dictionaries were trained for each class and concatenated, we evaluated the reconstruction quality of each
class-specific dictionary on its corresponding test split. The results are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:ksvd-reconstruction}.

\subsubsection{Dictionary Coherence}

The Normal block has the highest mutual incoherence of the three (0.868), while the Nodules block
is the lowest (0.511). This is likely because the Normal dictionary has to cover a much wider
range of healthy lung texture, so many atoms end up looking similar to each other. The Nodules
dictionary is smaller and each atom can specialise on a more specific local pattern, so there is
less redundancy between atoms.

\subsubsection{Classification and Localisation Performance}

A linear classifier trained on the K-SVD sparse codes reached 67\% patch-level accuracy (F1 = 0.64),
the lowest of the four dictionary-learning methods. Table~\ref{tab:ksvd-classification} gives the per-class breakdown.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\label{tab:ksvd-classification}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{F1-score} \\
\hline
Normal   & 0.62 & 0.80 & 0.70 \\
GGO      & 0.53 & 0.36 & 0.43 \\
Nodules  & 0.62 & 0.61 & 0.61 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Whole-volume localisation was poor: the Dice score dropped to about 0.004 once patch-level
predictions were combined into a full volume mask, with a hit rate of 0.008 and an IoU of 0.002.
This tells us that even though individual patches were classified reasonably well, the resulting
volume-level masks rarely lined up closely with the annotated lesions, likely because the patches
were coded independently without any spatial smoothing between neighbours.

\subsection{LC-KSVD}
\label{sec:lcksvd2-classification}

LC-KSVD2 trains a single 2592-atom dictionary jointly across classes, but the label-consistency
term partitions it into equally-sized, class-specific sub-dictionaries (864 atoms each) so that
each class's patches are preferentially coded by its own block. Table~\ref{tab:lcksvd2-reconstruction}
reports the mutual incoherence within each class block.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Mutual incoherence of the LC-KSVD2 class sub-dictionaries.}
\label{tab:lcksvd2-reconstruction}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Atoms} & \textbf{Mutual Incoherence} \\
\hline
Normal   & 864 & 0.193 \\
2c       & 864 & 0.247 \\
2d       & 864 & 0.261 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Class-block incoherence is noticeably lower than K-SVD's per-class blocks
(Table~\ref{tab:ksvd-reconstruction}, 0.51--0.87), which is expected: the label-consistency term
explicitly penalises a class's patches for activating atoms outside their own block, pushing each
block toward atoms that are distinguishable from the others. The joint training objective (reconstruction
+ label-consistency + classification error) converged from 3.818 at iteration 1 to 3.146 at
iteration 10; because this is a combined objective rather than a pure reconstruction term, its
magnitude is not directly comparable to the per-class errors reported for K-SVD.

The jointly-trained LC-KSVD model was evaluated on the same test split, with four classifier
heads trained on the same sparse-code representation to compare against the model's own built-in discriminative classifier.
Table \ref{tab:lcksvd2-classifiers} summarises patch-level performance across all four.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{LC-KSVD2 classifier-head comparison.}
    \label{tab:lcksvd2-classifiers}
    \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Classifier} & \textbf{Accuracy} & \textbf{Macro F1} & \textbf{2c F1} & \textbf{2d F1} \\
        \midrule
        LinearSVC (SVM)              & 0.852 & 0.69 & 0.44 & 0.70 \\
        Logistic Regression          & 0.751 & 0.57 & 0.38 & 0.48 \\
        Native LC-KSVD2 ($W$)        & 0.621 & 0.41 & 0.29 & 0.16 \\
        XGBoost                      & 0.614 & 0.48 & 0.32 & 0.38 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The externally-trained LinearSVC outperforms every other head, including LC-KSVD2's own built-in discriminative classifier $W$
(85.2\% vs.\ 62.1\% accuracy). The non-linear XGBoost do not improve on this either, both trailing
plain logistic regression, maybe because LC-KSVD learns to fit a linear classifier during training itself.
This suggests that the sparse-code feature space is already close to linearly separable for this task
and additional model capacity does not help.

\subsubsection{Whole-Volume Localisation}

When patch predictions were stitched back into full CT volumes, LC-KSVD2's localisation quality
fell sharply, giving a Dice score of only 0.010, a hit rate of 0.020, and an IoU of 0.005. This is
a much bigger drop than the patch-level classification numbers would suggest, and shows that good
patch-level accuracy does not automatically carry over to accurate spatial localisation once those
patches are combined back into a volume. Explainability scores were comparatively higher, with a
pointing-game accuracy of 0.88 and an energy-inside-mask score of 0.77, meaning the model's most
confident patches still tended to sit near the true lesion even where the overall mask did not
align well.

\subsection{FDDL}

FDDL also learns a 12096-atom dictionary split into equally-sized class blocks (4032 atoms
each), fit jointly under the Fisher discrimination criterion. Table~\ref{tab:fddl-reconstruction}
reports the mutual incoherence of each class block.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Mutual incoherence of the FDDL class sub-dictionaries.}
\label{tab:fddl-reconstruction}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Atoms} & \textbf{Mutual Incoherence} \\
\hline
Normal   & 4032 & 0.0182 \\
2c       & 4032 & 0.0186 \\
2d       & 4032 & 0.0186 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

FDDL's class blocks are an order of magnitude less mutually coherent than either K-SVD's
(Table~\ref{tab:ksvd-reconstruction}) or LC-KSVD2's (Table~\ref{tab:lcksvd2-reconstruction}), which
is consistent with the Fisher criterion directly minimising within-class scatter of the sparse
codes while maximising between-class scatter, rather than only penalising cross-class atom reuse
as LC-KSVD2's label-consistency term does. The joint training objective converged from
196{,}309.3 at iteration 1 to 153{,}820.2 at iteration 10; as with LC-KSVD2, this combined
objective (reconstruction + Fisher discrimination + coefficient terms) is on its own scale and is
not directly comparable in magnitude to K-SVD's pure reconstruction error.

\subsubsection{Classification and Localisation Performance}

A linear classifier trained on the FDDL sparse codes reached 78\% patch-level accuracy (F1 = 0.75),
sitting between K-SVD and Frozen Dictionary Learning. Table~\ref{tab:fddl-classification} shows the
per-class breakdown.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{FDDL patch-level classification performance (test split).}
\label{tab:fddl-classification}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{F1-score} \\
\hline
Normal   & 0.72 & 0.86 & 0.78 \\
GGO      & 0.60 & 0.47 & 0.53 \\
Nodules  & 0.72 & 0.71 & 0.72 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The low mutual incoherence of the FDDL blocks (Table~\ref{tab:fddl-reconstruction}) did not
translate into strong whole-volume localisation. Dice fell to 0.006, hit rate to 0.012 and IoU to
0.003 once patch predictions were assembled into full volumes, and explainability scores were
moderate (PG accuracy 0.72, EIM 0.64). This suggests that a well-separated sparse-code feature
space helps classification more than it helps spatial localisation, since the two use the codes
in quite different ways.

Section \ref{sec:lcksvd2-classification}). This suggests that building the
dictionary incrementally -- learning a stable normal-tissue base first and
adding each abnormality as a residual dictionary on top -- generalises to
unseen scans better than training a single dictionary jointly across all
three classes at once.

\subsection{Test-Set Classification Performance}
\label{sec:frozen-classification}

The patch-level results and confusion matrix reported above (Table~\ref{tab:frozen-classification}
and Figure~\ref{fig:frozen-confusion}) already cover the Frozen model's classification performance on the
44,384-patch test split. Frozen Dictionary Learning was the strongest of the four dictionary
methods on this task, reaching 85\% accuracy and an F1 score of 0.83, ahead of FDDL and well ahead
of plain K-SVD.

\subsection{Whole-Volume Localisation}

Whole-volume outputs were also produced by running the trained classifier over every patch
position in a scan and stitching the predictions back into a per-voxel abnormality map. These maps
were exported as overlay images and NIfTI volumes for visual review rather than scored against the
ground-truth masks directly, so no Dice or IoU figure is reported here for the full-volume case.
Visually, the abnormality maps picked up the general region of GGO and nodule findings in most
scans, though the boundaries were noticeably coarser than the original lesion outlines, which is
expected given the patches were classified independently of one another.

\subsection{Sub-Class Cluster Analysis}
\label{sec:cluster-results}

Applying the K-Means procedure of Section~\ref{sec:statistical-analysis} to the LC-KSVD2 sparse codes
of the 13,422 nodule (2d) patches with $K=4$ produced one large, dominant cluster and three much
smaller ones: 11,263 patches (83.9\%) fell into a single cluster, with the remaining three holding
6.5\%, 5.0\% and 4.5\% (inertia $=11{,}750.1$; 2-D PCA explained only 8.7\% of variance, reflecting how
spread out the 10,368-dimensional codes are). This imbalance suggests most nodule patches share a
generic sparse-code pattern, with the minority clusters plausibly capturing rarer sub-appearances
(e.g.\ size or calcification differences) that the joint nodule label does not distinguish.

Both scores are low in absolute terms (well short of the $\sim$0.5 usually taken to indicate

even though Section~\ref{sec:frozen-classification} found the Frozen dictionary stronger for
classification.

The top 30 atoms ranked by variance across the four LC-KSVD2 GGO centroids each activate a visibly
different subset per cluster rather than one shared profile at different scales -- indicating the
sparse codes still capture genuine sub-population structure at the atom level, even though the
clusters overlap enough to keep the silhouette score low.

\section{Machine Learning}

The raw-patch baselines described in Section~\ref{sec:ml-baseline-methods} were evaluated on the
same test split used for LC-KSVD2 Table~\ref{tab:raw-patch-classifiers}
summarises per-class performance for the three classifiers trained on the
full 864-dimension raw patch.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{Raw-patch classifier comparison, per class for the test split}
    \label{tab:raw-patch-classifiers}
    \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Classifier} & \textbf{Accuracy} & \textbf{Normal F1} & \textbf{2c F1} & \textbf{2d F1} \\
        \midrule
        Logistic Regression         & 0.780 & 0.90 & 0.49 & 0.13 \\
        LinearSVC (MaxAbsScaler)    & 0.854 & 0.93 & 0.53 & 0.12 \\
        XGBoost                     & 0.832 & 0.92 & 0.54 & 0.31 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Overall accuracy for the best raw-patch model, LinearSVC at 85.4\%, is close
to LC-KSVD2's sparse-code LinearSVC at 85.2\% (Table
\ref{tab:lcksvd2-classifiers}) .However, accuracy on this test split is dominated by the
majority normal class, so this comparison is misleading. 
Computing the same macro F1 used to evaluate LC-KSVD2 gives a
starkly different picture: the raw-patch LinearSVC reaches a macro F1 of
only 0.53 (normal 0.93, 2c 0.53, 2d 0.12), well below LC-KSVD2's sparse-code
LinearSVC at 0.69. The gap is most severe on the nodule class specifically:
the raw-patch LinearSVC's 2d F1 of 0.12 (precision 0.17, recall 0.10) means
it fails to reliably detect nodules from raw intensities alone, compared to
an F1 of 0.70 for the same classifier trained on sparse codes. The
non-linear tree baselines (XGBoost) do somewhat better
on the nodule class than the raw-patch linear models (2d F1 of 0.33 and 0.31
respectively), but still fall well short of the sparse-code LinearSVC.

The two-feature (mean HU, std HU) ablation reached 80.8\% accuracy and a 2d
F1 of 0.21 with Logistic Regression, and 86.5\% accuracy with a 2d F1 of
0.23 with LinearSVC . Capturing a substantial fraction of the full
raw-patch models' accuracy from coarse intensity statistics alone, but with
nodule-class performance no better than the full-patch raw baselines. Taken
together, these results indicate that classifying raw patch intensities,
whether the full 864-dimension patch or just its two summary statistics,
recovers most of the normal-vs-abnormal signal but is a substantially worse
representation than the learned sparse code for distinguishing the two
abnormality classes from each other, particularly the smaller and rarer
nodule class consistent with the dictionary learning and sparse coding
stages contributing meaningfully beyond what raw intensity alone provides.

These raw-patch models were only evaluated on classification. Unlike the dictionary-learning
methods, they were not carried through to whole-volume localisation, so no segmentation or
explainability scores are reported for the Machine Learning group.

\section{Grad-CAM}

The token-based Grad-CAM variant described in Section~\ref{sec:grad-cam}, using the predicted abnormality
logit as the target score, was evaluated on the full test set using the metrics:
Dice, IoU, Attribution IoU, pointing-game hit rate, and energy inside the ground-truth mask.
Table~\ref{tab:ctclip-gradcam-metrics} reports the resulting mean and standard deviation.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \caption{CT-CLIP Grad-CAM localisation metrics evaluated on the full test set}
    \label{tab:ctclip-gradcam-metrics}
    \begin{tabular}{lc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Value} \\
        \midrule
        Dice                    & 0.0019 $\pm$ 0.0034 \\
        IoU                     & 0.0009 $\pm$ 0.0017 \\
        Attribution IoU         & 0.0009 $\pm$ 0.0017 \\
        Pointing-game hit rate  & 0.0000 $\pm$ 0.0000 \\
        Energy inside mask      & 0.0008 $\pm$ 0.0018 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every metric is close to zero meaning that the voxel with the highest heatmap value did not fall inside the
annotated lesion in any case in the test set.
The ground-truth annotations cover an average of 59{,}443 voxels out of 55{,}296{,}000 voxels in a volume.
In comparison, even the most selective of the four candidate thresholds
($95^{\text{th}}$ percentile) marks 2{,}764{,}800 voxels, corresponding to
5\% of the volume. Several cases achieved their best Dice score at the
$70^{\text{th}}$ or $80^{\text{th}}$ percentile, resulting in 20--30\% of the
volume being marked as positive.

Therefore, the thresholded Grad-CAM explanation is often much larger than the
small lesion it is intended to localise. This large size difference strongly
reduces the overlap-based metrics and contributes to the very low Dice and IoU
scores. However, the zero pointing-game hit rate also indicates that the
strongest individual activation did not reliably fall within the annotated
lesion. Thus, the quantitative results suggest that the Grad-CAM heatmaps were
not sufficiently precise for localising the small target lesions.
Figure~\ref{fig:ctclip-gradcam-nodule-example} shows the Grad-CAM heatmap for a single nodule cases from the test set.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Images/Chapter 4/gradcam-nodule-example.png}
    \caption[Grad-CAM heatmap visualization]{Grad-CAM for 3 example cases: CT slice (left), ground-truth
    nodules (centre), and Grad-CAM heatmap (right).}
    \label{fig:ctclip-gradcam-nodule-example}
\end{figure}

We can observe, the strongest heatmap activations were spread broadly across
the chest wall and even outside the lung rather than being concentrated around the three
annotated nodule boundaries. The single highest-valued voxel also fell outside
all three ground-truth annotations, resulting in a pointing-game miss.

This example is consistent with the overall evaluation: the Grad-CAM heatmap
generally focuses on the lung region rather than the image background, but it
is not precise enough to localise a specific small lesion within the surrounding
lung tissue.

\subsection{Recommendations and Verbal feedback}

In addition to standard questions, the radiologist provided verbatim free-text comments which are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative-feedback}.
The consulting radiologist emphasised two practical points: (1) the dataset's ground-truth masks are sometimes annotated conservatively as
broader regions that include adjacent tissue, and (2) the model's predicted masks were, in many inspected cases, more tightly localised to the
visually apparent abnormality. Taken together, these observations suggest that simple overlap metrics (Dice/IoU against the provided masks)
may under-estimate clinically relevant localisation performance when the reference annotations are intentionally broad. We therefore report the
qualitative reviewer finding as an important complement to the quantitative metrics and recommend future work include either more precise
annotations or a radiologist-driven refinement loop to produce a higher-fidelity reference set.
